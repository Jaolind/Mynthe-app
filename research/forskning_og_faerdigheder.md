# Research and developmentally appropriate skills: how 5-7-year-olds learn from apps and digital games (Danish angle)

Method note: about 18 web searches and fetches, run 14 Nov 2026. Several primary sites could not be fetched from this session (sst.dk and news.psu.edu were outside the session's network allowlist). Some findings below therefore rest on search-result summaries of the primary documents, and these are flagged. Items marked "(foundational, pre-2015)" are older classics.

## (a) When and how do educational apps produce learning for 5-7-year-olds?

### Takeaway
On average, educational apps produce small-to-moderate learning gains (about +0.3 to +0.4 SD). The gains are largest for narrow, "constrained" skills (letters, counting, number facts) measured with researcher-made tests. They are smaller in randomized trials and on standardized or broader measures. Quality varies enormously between apps. The best predictor of quality is the Four Pillars: active, engaged (not distracted), meaningful, socially interactive, plus a clear learning goal. Most top-selling "educational" apps score poorly on these.

### Cited Findings
**Framework: the Four Pillars (Hirsh-Pasek et al., 2015)**
- Hirsh-Pasek, Zosh, Golinkoff, Gray, Robb and Kaufman (2015), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 16(1), 3-34. The paper argues that children learn best when they are (1) actively involved ("minds-on", not just tapping), (2) engaged without distraction, (3) working with meaningful content linked to their lives, and (4) socially interactive. All of this must sit within a well-defined learning goal ("educational context"). Without a learning goal, an app with all four pillars counts as entertainment, not education — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html); [New America summary](https://www.newamerica.org/insights/new-framework-identify-truly-educational-apps/)
- Apps that include all four pillars are "more likely to lead to significant learning", especially when they support scaffolded exploration, questioning and discovery tied to clear goals. The authors propose a grid that crosses a Pillar Score with Educational Context — [EdTech Hub record](https://docs.edtechhub.org/lib/I73DRP3M); [ScienceDaily 2015](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/04/150420123021.htm)
- Context: in January 2015 Apple's App Store had about 80,000 "educational" apps, largely unregulated and untested — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html)

**App-store quality audit (Meyer et al., 2021)**
- Meyer, Zosh, McLaren, Robb, McCaffery, Golinkoff, Hirsh-Pasek and Radesky (2021), *Journal of Children and Media*. Each app was coded 0-3 on each pillar. The sample was the top 100 children's educational apps on Google Play and Apple, plus the 24 apps most played by preschoolers. A total score of 4 or below counted as low quality, and scores were low across all pillars — [paper PDF](https://kathyhirshpasek.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2021/03/How-educational-are-educational-apps-for-young-children-App-store-content-analysis-using-the-Four-Pillars-of-Learning-framework.pdf); [Brookings blog](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/looking-for-an-educational-life-raft-apps-may-not-be-the-answer/)
- Free apps scored lower than paid apps on some criteria, especially "engagement". The reason was disruptive ads, irrelevant rewards, and visual and sound effects that pull attention away from learning — [The Sector summary](https://thesector.com.au/?p=22850); [Futurity](https://www.futurity.org/education-apps-children-parents-2563592/)
- Recommendation: designers should work with child-development experts, and app stores should provide evidence-based ratings — [Futurity](https://www.futurity.org/education-apps-children-parents-2563592/)
- Note: the brief's term "Learn-o-meter" did not appear in any source I found. I could not verify it as a named Meyer et al. instrument (see Gaps).

**Meta-analyses and systematic reviews**
- Kim, Gilbert, Yu and Gale (2021), "Measures Matter", *AERA Open*. It pooled 36 intervention studies and 285 effect sizes, preschool to grade 3. Average effect was +0.31 SD, similar for math and literacy. Effects were larger for preschoolers than for K-3, for researcher-developed tests than for standardized tests, and for constrained skills (e.g. letter knowledge, counting) than for unconstrained skills (e.g. vocabulary, comprehension). The average masks large variation between apps — [ERIC full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1323865.pdf); [Harvard CEPR](https://cepr.harvard.edu/resource/measures-matter-meta-analysis-effects-educational-apps-preschool-grade-3-childrens-0)
- Gilbert, Young and Kim (2026), EdWorkingPaper (an update). It covers 141 studies and 841 effect sizes for ages 3-9, pooled d = 0.40 with substantial heterogeneity. The same moderators hold: researcher-made and constrained-skill outcomes show larger effects. App dosage and app quality ratings did not predict effect size. RCTs and larger studies show smaller effects. Evidence at scale and with long-term follow-up remains limited — [EdWorkingPapers ai26-1579](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai26-1579)
- Griffith et al. (2020), *Pediatrics*, is a systematic review (not a meta-analysis) of 35 studies (n = 4,639) of children under 6. It found evidence of learning benefits from interactive apps for early academic skills, particularly early math in typically developing children. Of the four math-app studies with control groups, three favored app users. Risk of bias was often unclear — [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/pediatrics/20191223/apps-show-teaching-potential-for-math-early-academic-skills); [FIU](https://digitalcommons.fiu.edu/psychology_fac/36)
- Benavides-Varela et al. (2020), a meta-analysis of digital interventions for struggling math learners, reports a mean effect of +0.54 (cited via secondary source, not verified in the original) — [Outhwaite et al. 2023 GEM background paper, UCL](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10174240/1/Outhwaite%20et%20al.%202023%20GEM%20background%20paper.pdf)

**Strong single trial: math apps (onebillion), age 4-6**
- Outhwaite, Faulder, Gulliford and Pitchford (2019 pub.; 2018 online), *Journal of Educational Psychology*, was an RCT with 389 UK children aged 4-5. Children used math apps for 30 minutes a day over 12 weeks, either as a supplement to or a replacement for a small-group activity. Both groups outperformed standard teaching, with no difference between supplement and replacement — [Nottingham eprints](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/52319); [Birmingham](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/raising-early-achievement-in-math-with-interactive-apps-a-randomi)
- An EEF-funded trial (about 1,200 children aged 5-6, 120 English schools) gave lower-attaining children onebillion for 30 minutes a day, 4 days a week for 12 weeks. It reports about +3 months' additional progress (vendor page; treat with caution) — [onebillion evidence](https://onebillion.org/impact/evidence); [University of Nottingham press release 2017](https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/news/pressreleases/2017/june/addition-of-new-maths-app-equals-better-learning-for-children.aspx)

**Distracting enhancements (digital storybooks)**
- Takacs, Swart and Bus (2015), *Review of Educational Research*, meta-analysis. Technology-enhanced stories can beat print, but the effect depends on the features. Interactive features such as hotspots and games, whether relevant or not, were detrimental to story comprehension. Story-congruent multimedia (animation or camera movement that directs attention to narrated details, matching music and sound) helped — [SAGE PDF](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.3102/0034654314566989); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4647204)
- Bus and colleagues conclude that, without good design, digital books are inferior to print, but high-quality e-books can outperform print. Story-unrelated mini-games overwhelm children (cognitive overload) — [Edinburgh research portal](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/the-promise-of-multimedia-enhancement-in-childrens-digital-storyb/); [Leiden thesis](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item:2872126/view)

### Inferences
- For a 5-7 product, the most evidence-backed bet is drill and practice with adaptive scaffolding for constrained skills (letter-sound links, phoneme blending, counting, number line, number bonds to 10/20). Claims of effects on "broad" skills (comprehension, creativity, problem-solving) need much more caution.
- The finding that dosage does not predict effect suggests that short, focused sessions (10-20 minutes) are defensible. More time does not equal more learning.
- Every feature should pass a test: "Is it congruent with the learning goal?" Story-unrelated mini-games, hotspots, ads and celebratory effects should be removed. This is also a clear differentiator, since most market apps fail it.
- Independent evaluation with standardized measures (e.g. a Danish RCT with a research partner) would be a strong credibility signal, given that RCTs show smaller effects.

### Gaps
- "Learn-o-meter": not found in any source. It may be a misremembered name. The Four Pillars coding scheme from Meyer et al. 2021 is the verified instrument.
- I did not find a Danish or Nordic RCT of literacy apps for 0.-1. klasse (e.g. evaluations of GraphoGame in Danish) within this budget. Recommend a follow-up search ("GraphoGame dansk", "Nationalt Videncenter for Læsning apps").
- Joint media engagement (Takeuchi and Stevens 2011, foundational, pre-2015) and parent co-play studies were not specifically searched. The Four Pillars "social interaction" pillar and AAP's co-viewing advice support the idea, but no effect size was collected.

## (b) Screen-time guidance (WHO, AAP, Sundhedsstyrelsen, Danish commissions, 2025-2026)

### Takeaway
International guidance for ages 5-7 has moved away from fixed hour limits, toward the quality of content, what screens displace (sleep, movement, play, relationships), co-use and platform design. WHO's one-hour cap applies only to under-5s. AAP's January 2026 statement dropped hour caps and blames engagement-maximizing design. Sundhedsstyrelsen (2023) sets no time limits but advises against screens before bedtime and urges parents to watch their own use. The Danish Trivselskommission (Feb 2025) recommended no personal smartphone or tablet before age 13 and mobile bans in schools and SFO. That is a significant headwind for tablet-based products aimed at private family use.

### Cited Findings
**WHO (2019)**
- WHO's first guidelines for under-5s: no screen time under age 1. For ages 2-4, no more than one hour of sedentary screen time a day, and "less is better". There is no WHO screen limit specific to 5-7-year-olds in these guidelines — [PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/health/un-recommends-no-screen-time-for-babies-only-1-hour-for-kids-under-5); [EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/world-health-organization-recommends-no-more-than-one-hour-of-screen-time-for-most-children-under-5/2019/04)

**AAP (2016 → 2026)**
- 2016: avoid media for under 18-24 months (except video chat). For ages 2-5, limit to one hour a day of high-quality programming, co-viewed where possible — [EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/world-health-organization-recommends-no-more-than-one-hour-of-screen-time-for-most-children-under-5/2019/04)
- 20 January 2026: AAP policy statement "Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents" in *Pediatrics*, plus a technical report. It drops the fixed daily limit and treats media as an ecosystem (TV, internet, social media, games, apps, AI, interactive devices). It focuses on what media displaces (sleep, outdoor time, in-person contact) — [EdSurge 5 Feb 2026](https://edsurge.com/news/2026-02-05-new-aap-screen-time-recommendations-focus-less-on-screens-more-on-family-time); [Contemporary Pediatrics 29 Jan 2026](https://www.contemporarypediatrics.com/view/aap-calls-for-system-level-changes-to-improve-children-s-digital-media-environments); [Mount Sinai Parenting](https://parenting.mountsinai.org/tip-of-the-week/meet-the-digital-ecosystem)
- AAP 2026 holds that engagement-maximizing platform design harms children. It calls for child-centered design and shared responsibility (families, clinicians, industry, society) — [EPIC](https://epic.org/in-new-policy-statement-american-academy-of-pediatrics-sounds-alarm-about-impact-of-harmful-platform-design-for-kids/); [ConnectSafely](https://connectsafely.org/digital-ecosystems/)
- Note: EdSurge describes the 2016 AAP as "two hours a day" for older children. The 2016 guidance for 2-5-year-olds was one hour. Sources differ in emphasis (regulation versus family tools).

**Sundhedsstyrelsen (Denmark, October 2023)**
- In October 2023 SST published new recommendations on screen use: four for children, young people and adults, and four extra for parents with children living at home. Among them: no screens before bedtime, and parents should be aware of their own screen use when with their child, since it can harm the relationship. Children under 2 should not use screens without active interaction with an adult. These recommendations underpin the "Styr på skærm" campaign — [SST background note 06.10.2023](https://www.sst.dk/-/media/Udgivelser/2023/Skaerm/Baggrundsnotat---Sundhedsstyrelsens-anbefalinger-om-skaermbrug-06102023.ashx); [SST recommendations](https://www.sst.dk/-/media/Borger/Søvn/SKAERM_SST_ANBEFALINGER.ashx); [Mobilsiden](https://mobilsiden.dk/?p=196839) (search-snippet basis; the PDF could not be fetched from this session)
- SST deliberately did not set time limits, unlike WHO — [SDU/SIF: current recommendations](https://www.sdu.dk/da/sif/forskning/projekter/det_digitale_barn_forside/nuvaerende_anbefalinger_om_skaermbrug)
- In 2024 SST followed up with a note giving advice on how to follow the recommendations — [SST note 2024](https://sundhedsstyrelsen.dk/-/media/Udgivelser/2024/Skærm/Notat-Raad-til-at-efterleve-anbefalingerne-vedr-skaermbrug.ashx)
- SDU/SIF has a 2024 report on screen use among 4-year-olds in Denmark (a relevant baseline; contents not reviewed) — [SIF 2024](https://www.sdu.dk/da/sif/rapporter/2024/skaermbrug_blandt_4_aarige_boern_i_danmark)

**Trivselskommissionen (February 2025)**
- The report had 35 recommendations. They include: no personal smartphone or tablet for children before 13 (a "basic phone" as the alternative, and not by law); a mobile ban in primary schools, SFO and after-school clubs; "adults as digital role models"; and pressure on the EU for stricter regulation of digital services. Researchers and pupils criticized parts of it — [Altinget](https://www.altinget.dk/boern/artikel/mindre-skaermtid-og-flere-fritidsaktiviteter-saadan-vil-trivselskommissionen-styrke-unges-trivsel); [TV2 Kosmopol](https://www.tv2kosmopol.dk/metropolen/anbefaler-smartphone-forbud-frem-til-13-ar); [Altinget, researcher criticism](https://www.altinget.dk/digital/artikel/forsker-mobilforbud-er-en-mistillidserklaering-til-elever-foraeldre-og-fagprofessionelle)
- Municipalities are following up with local recommendations (e.g. Frederiksberg, "Sammen om børn og unges trivsel") — [Frederiksberg PDF](https://backoffice.frederiksberg.dk/media/zjzax0n4/anbefalinger-sammen-om-boern-og-unges-trivsel.pdf)

### Inferences
- Under 2026 guidance, a product for 5-7-year-olds is not "illegitimate", but it must show that it (1) does not displace sleep, movement or relationships: short sessions, natural stopping points, no use at bedtime; (2) supports co-use; and (3) has none of the engagement-maximizing design that AAP 2026 and Radesky criticize.
- The Trivselskommission's "no own tablet before 13" message means Danish parents will be skeptical. Positioning as a shared family tablet or the parent's phone, with co-play, short sessions and a clear learning goal, fits the Danish discourse better than "the child's own app world".
- A Danish product could reasonably adopt SST's recommendations as a design principle (e.g. a built-in "not before bedtime" lock and a parent dashboard).

### Gaps
- I did not obtain SST's exact wording of all eight recommendations, because sst.dk was blocked in this session. It should be fetched directly.
- "Kulturministeriet" (Ministry of Culture) recommendations: the Trivselskommission referred to the government. I did not separately verify whether Kulturministeriet issued its own 2024-2025 guidance (e.g. on children's digital media or age limits). Possible 2025-2026 follow-up, such as the government's political agreement on age verification and a social-media age limit of 15 (autumn 2025), was not verified within this search budget.
- WHO has not, as far as found, updated its guidance for 5-7-year-olds. A blog claimed "2024 WHO update"; it was not confirmed.

## (c) Concrete learning goals per domain at age 5-7 (0.-1. klasse) and evidence-based mechanics

### Takeaway
The Danish framework for børnehaveklassen (0. klasse) covers language (letter shapes, names and sounds), mathematical awareness (number symbols, counting, geometric language in everyday life) and four further areas. Fælles Mål remain binding (competence goals) until the new "fagplaner" take effect in the 2027/28 school year. Danish has an opaque orthography: Danish first-graders reached only about 80% word-reading accuracy versus over 90% in transparent languages. Danish learners therefore benefit from systematic, explicit training of the most frequent and regular letter-sound links first, plus phonological awareness. In design terms the evidence points to: adaptive difficulty and scaffolding, immediate informative feedback, few distractions, rewards tied to the task rather than external lures, and no manipulative "dark patterns".

### Cited Findings
**Danish curriculum framework**
- The børnehaveklasse competence areas are Language, Mathematical awareness, Natural phenomena, and Creative and musical forms of expression. Lex.dk lists six areas in total, adding body and movement, and engagement and community — [UVM: Fælles Mål børnehaveklassen (2019 version, 2024 publication)](https://uvm.dk/-/media/filer/uvm/udd/folke/pdf24/maj/240513-faellesmaal-boernehaveklassen.pdf); [lex.dk børnehaveklasse](https://lex.dk/b%C3%B8rnehaveklasse)
- Language: the attention points include linguistic awareness, e.g. recognizing the shape, name and sound of the letters (except q, w, x, z) — [UVM børnehaveklassen](https://uvm.dk/-/media/filer/uvm/udd/folke/pdf24/maj/240513-faellesmaal-boernehaveklassen.pdf)
- Mathematical awareness: the child can use numbers and geometric language in everyday situations. An attention point is recognizing and ordering number symbols and using them to count — [UVM børnehaveklassen](https://uvm.dk/-/media/filer/uvm/udd/folke/pdf24/maj/240513-faellesmaal-boernehaveklassen.pdf)
- The børnehaveklasse executive order (bekendtgørelse) took effect on 1 January 2025. Competence goals are guiding targets for the end of 0. klasse, and teaching is at least 600 hours — [Lovtidende](https://www.lovtidende.dk/api/pdf/247006)
- Since 1 January 2025, competence areas and competence goals are binding. For Danish, mathematics and børnehaveklassen, the attention points remain binding too — [emu.dk Dansk faghæfte](https://emu.dk/grundskole/dansk/faghaefte-faelles-maal-laeseplan-og-vejledning)
- Mathematics competence areas: mathematical competences, number and algebra, geometry and measurement, statistics and probability — [emu.dk Matematik](https://www.emu.dk/grundskole/matematik/faghaefte-faelles-maal-laeseplan-og-vejledning?b=t5-t9); [UVM Fælles Mål matematik](https://www.uvm.dk/-/media/filer/uvm/udd/folke/pdf24/maj/240513-faellesmaal-matematik.pdf)
- The subject renewal ("Fagfornyelsen"): new, simplified subject plans are being trialed in 2025-2026. From the 2027/2028 school year, Fælles Mål will be replaced by the folkeskole's subject plans — [STUK: Grundskolen](https://stukuvm.dk/udvikling-af-fag-og-uddannelser/grundskolen/); [lex.dk Fælles Mål](https://lex.dk/F%C3%A6lles_M%C3%A5l)
- A Dansk Audiologopædi critique argued that an earlier (2009) version gave phoneme awareness too little weight — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/269195592.pdf)
- Fælles Mål for English and Danish as a second language are also available (relevant for early English from 1. klasse) — [UVM engelsk](https://www.uvm.dk/-/media/filer/uvm/udd/folke/pdf24/maj/240513-faellesmaal-engelsk.pdf); [UVM DSA](https://www.uvm.dk/-/media/filer/uvm/udd/folke/pdf24/maj/240513-faellesmaal-dansk-som-andetsprog-supplerende.pdf)

**Danish orthography (opaque) and reading acquisition**
- Seymour, Aro and Erskine (2003), *British Journal of Psychology* (foundational, pre-2015; Elbro was the Danish representative in the network). After about one year of instruction, word-reading accuracy was over 90% in most European orthographies, but not in Portuguese, French, Danish and especially English. Danish sat around 80%. Deep orthographies require a dual (logographic and alphabetic) foundation that takes more than twice as long to establish — [Seymour, Aro and Erskine PDF](https://eclass-b.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/PPP363/Seymour_Aro_Erskine.pdf); [Ellis et al., orthographic depth](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~ncellis/NickEllis/Publications_files/Orthographic%20Depth%20Reading.pdf)
- Elbro's Danish studies: the most frequent, productive and reliable grapheme-phoneme associations were learned first, in the form of single letters (secondary source) — [PMC6328448](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6328448)

**Mechanics: rewards, motivation and manipulative design**
- Radesky et al. (2022), *JAMA Network Open*, studied the apps used by 160 children aged 3-5. 80% of apps had manipulative design, and nearly 99% of children met at least one such feature in their most-used apps. The categories were parasocial pressure (a character pleading "save me!"), time pressure (countdowns), navigation constraints, and lures (stickers, trophies, accumulating daily rewards). Prevalence was higher in families with lower parental education. Young children cannot tell a sales screen from the game, or see that time pressure is fabricated, and the tricks contribute to family conflict over handing back the device — [Michigan Medicine](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/design-tricks-commonly-used-monetize-young-childrens-app-use); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/956153); [Deceptive Design](https://deceptive.design/articles/prevalence-and-characteristics-of-manipulative-design-in-mobile-applications-used-by-children)
- Overjustification: external rewards can lower intrinsic motivation (Deci, Koestner and Ryan; foundational). Research on educational games points to the risk being greatest when the task is already interesting, when rewards are used controllingly, and for young children. A Springer study tests external rewards in children's game play, with initial interest as a moderator, and Fu et al. (2014, *Computers & Education*) studied external rewards in an educational game for primary pupils. There is little empirical research specifically on badges for young children — [Springer, Current Psychology 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-022-02905-6); [MDPI Education Sciences 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/13/7/668); [IGI badges](https://igi-global.com/article/designing-digital-badges-for-educational-games/223115)
- MIT's Scratch team deliberately avoids points and badges, to protect intrinsic motivation and creativity — [MIT Lifelong Kindergarten, gamification chapter](https://lcl.media.mit.edu/resources/readings/chapter3-gamification.pdf)
- Irrelevant rewards and sound and visual effects lower the "engagement" pillar score (Meyer et al. 2021) — [The Sector](https://thesector.com.au/?p=22850)

### Inferences
Proposed learning goals per domain, ages 5-7 (0.-1. klasse). These are inferences combining Fælles Mål, the orthography research and the meta-analysis finding that constrained skills respond best to apps:
- **Phonological awareness:** rhyme, syllable splitting, first sound, then phoneme blending and segmentation (2-4 sounds). This is a strong candidate for apps (constrained, trainable).
- **Letter-sound correspondence (Danish):** start with frequent, regular letter-sound links and consonants with stable pronunciation (e.g. m, s, l, r, a, i). Introduce vowel variation (e.g. long and short vowels, "e"/"æ") and silent letters ("hv", "d" in "mand") later. Because of the opacity, this needs more repetitions and explicit training than in Finnish or Italian. All 29 letters, with the ending focus on q, w, x, z being optional (in line with the 0. klasse attention point).
- **High-frequency or irregular words ("sight words"):** in Danish a small set of very frequent irregular words (e.g. "jeg", "det", "er", "de", "og", "hun") is a sensible complement, given the dual foundation Seymour describes. No Danish-specific list was verified.
- **Number sense:** 0. klasse: counting and ordering number symbols to 20, one-to-one correspondence, subitizing, comparing quantities, number bonds to 10. 1. klasse: number line to 100, addition and subtraction within 20, tens and ones. The math-app RCTs (onebillion) support this domain best.
- **Geometry and patterns:** shape names and geometric language in everyday situations (curriculum), patterns and sequencing.
- **Executive function, fine motor, SEL, English:** I collected no app-specific evidence (see Gaps). Recommend these as secondary goals with caution in claims.

Mechanics, ordered by strength of evidence:
1. A clear learning goal per activity, and removal of goal-incongruent effects, hotspots and mini-games (Takacs et al.; Meyer et al.).
2. Adaptive difficulty and scaffolding (the Four Pillars "scaffolded exploration"; onebillion-type apps).
3. Task-related, informative feedback instead of trophies, stickers or daily streaks (Radesky; overjustification).
4. No timers, pleading characters, ads or purchase lures (Radesky; AAP 2026).
5. Co-play and conversation prompts for adults ("socially interactive" pillar; SST's focus on the parent-child relationship).
6. Short sessions with natural stopping points, and no autoplay to the next level.

Spaced repetition and mastery learning are well documented in general learning research. I did not collect sources for them specific to ages 5-7 within this budget, so they should be cited as general principles, not age-specific evidence.
